The Baker's Dozen

A 13-state restart variant of M12

Status: proved in Coq, on top of Jeff Ketchersid's Coq development for M12. Superseded on 28 October 2026 by M13b from Jeff Ketchersid and Argon, which has a larger proved bound. See Verification.

The other 22 of M12's 24 slots, including the whole W engine, are M12's, unchanged.

The Baker's Dozen is a 13-state variant of M12, a 12-state Busy Beaver machine by Jeff Ketchersid and Gemini. M12 keeps three numbers on its tape, a level, an accumulator and a countdown. Each step uses up three units of the countdown and, through an engine of fast-growing functions, raises the level enormously. A round runs steps until fewer than three units are left. M12 runs one large round and halts. The Baker's Dozen changes one of M12's transitions and sends M12's halt slot to a new state. Where M12 builds its countdown, the new machine restarts. It reads the whole tape as a new, much larger countdown and starts over. It runs four large rounds, then halts. The name is a nod to its parent, since a baker's dozen is twelve plus one.

In the fast-growing hierarchy its count of ones is provably more than fω+24(fω(fω(4))), which is fω+2 applied four times. That is still level ω+2, the same level as M12 with more iterations, not ω+3. The bound is above the proved lower bounds of M12 and of the 14-state M14, also credited to Jeff Ketchersid and Gemini (see Size).

How it works

How M12 works

This part is M12's, as Jeff Ketchersid's write-up and Coq proof describe it. M12 keeps its three numbers on the tape as blocks (Diagram 2). On the left is the level p, a stack of p “10” pairs. Next comes the accumulator, a run of 1s. On the right is the countdown d, a row of d pairs. The head works between the accumulator and the countdown.

The engine is what makes the numbers large. Run at level p, it turns an input b into Wp(b), where W0(b) = b + 3 and Wj+1(b) is Wj applied b + 1 times to 2. So Wj grows like fj, and the higher the level, the stronger the engine. The engine's slots, apart from the exit and the bottom-of-stack slot, already appear in Racheline's 21-state machine of 2024.

The level and the accumulator feed each other. An engine call runs the engine on the input 1, keeps the output as the new accumulator and adds half the old accumulator to the level. The level keeps growing, so every call runs the engine at a higher level than the one before. A step has three parts, with Coq names in parentheses.

  • Start a step (C_REL) spends one unit of the countdown and sets up m engine calls, where the accumulator is 2m + 1. It also lowers the level by one and refills the accumulator from the engine.
  • Engine call (CE_EVEN) then runs m times.
  • Finish a step (D_STEP) spends two more units of the countdown and passes the accumulator through the engine.

A round is ⌊d/3⌋ steps. After every round but the last, M12 builds a new countdown from the accumulator. Only its last round is large. Diagram 3 puts these loops inside the Baker's Dozen's restart and shows how large each one is.

The restart

Each round of the Baker's Dozen leaves r = d mod 3 units of countdown. If r = 0, the halt check (G_HALT) stops the machine with p + m + 3 ones, where 2m + 1 is the accumulator. If r = 1, a last step starts but cannot finish. If r = 2, it starts and the pad (D1) folds the last unit into the accumulator. Either way the machine reaches the point where M12 builds a new countdown.

From there M12 sweeps left in states K and L, rewriting the accumulator's 1s as 10 pairs, its new countdown. At the stack, L0 = 0LH ends the sweep and calls the engine. In the Baker's Dozen, L0 = 1RL lets the sweep run on through the stack, which is already 10 pairs, to the blank left end. There K0 = 0LM and M0 = 0LG hand control to G. The whole tape is now one countdown. The run that ends the start-up (START) turns its first four pairs into a new level 7 and accumulator 3, leaving p + k − 1 pairs (Diagram 2, bottom row). That is the restart (RST). Since p is huge after a round, so is the new countdown.

The halt check is the same sweep. With r = 0 the accumulator is odd, so K, not L, reaches the stack. K0 = 0LM steps onto a 1 and M halts on the undefined M1.

Why it stops after four rounds

The engine has a simple form modulo powers of 3. At every level j ≥ 2, 2Wj(b) + 5 is a power of 3. Every engine call inside a round runs at level 5 or more, so all their outputs are ≡ 38 (mod 81). With W's parity, that makes the first step of a round add 0 to the level mod 3 and every later step add 1. So after a round of n = ⌊d/3⌋ steps from level 7, the new countdown is ≡ n + 1 (mod 3). In base 3, n is d without its last digit, so each round uses up one digit.

Redone modulo 81, the same sums take the start-up's countdown of 5 to four large countdowns ≡ 32 (mod 81), 14 (mod 27), 8 (mod 9) and 0 (mod 3). So rounds 0 to 3 restart and round 4 halts.

Size

Coq proves that the Baker's Dozen halts with more than fω+24(fω(fω(4))) ones, and also gives the exact number as an expression in the four round outputs. The proof reuses the bound lemmas of Jeff Ketchersid's Coq development for M12 unchanged, plus two small new lemmas. One says W grows with the level, and the other turns iterates of fω+1 into fω+2.

Verification

  • Coq 8.20.1 with busycoq proves that the Baker's Dozen halts from the blank tape, gives its exact count of ones, proves fω+24(fω(fω(4))) < σ, and proves that σ is above the proved bounds of M12 and M14. Every theorem is closed under the global context, with no axioms. The proof is in bb13/coq, and its build script fetches Jeff Ketchersid's files and checks everything in under 20 seconds.
  • A probe checks the compiled transition table against the machine string, entry by entry.
  • Jeff Ketchersid's lemmas for the shared rules and for the bound go through unchanged on the new table, and every copied block is checked to match his files exactly.
  • Before the Coq proof, our own symbolic prover and two independent simulations gave the same rules and the same four-round run.
