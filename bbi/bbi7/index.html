This page sorts every seven-instruction Turing machine in Tree Normal Form by shape, the number of states and symbols it uses, so that each cell can be settled on its own. Our own program runs a cell and a second, independent check confirms the result. Every colour, count and statement here comes from that calculation alone. A † marks what others found.

Each square gives a cell's shape in states × symbols and its number of machines. A checked square also gives any holdouts left and the steps of its longest checked halter, and its name links to the cell's page. A + marks a count that may grow through shorter machines the census left undecided. The row and column numbers in bold give the box.

The census

The census counts 305,333,128 seven-instruction machines, each the child of a machine that halted within 4,000,000 steps. It left 13,654 shorter machines undecided, 14 with four instructions, 424 with five and 13,216 with six, and machines grown from them are not counted, which is what a + marks. So there are at least 305,333,128.

What counts

A halter is run to its halt, a non-halter carries a cycler or translated-cycler certificate (Coq-BB5's Loop decider), an n-gram CPS certificate, a repeated-word-list certificate or a finite-automata-reduction certificate, and any other machine stays a holdout. A cell shows as open or resolved only once a second program, written from CHECKING.md alone, agrees on its count, every halt and every certificate. The results are informal, in the community's sense. A † marks a number credited to others. It is shown as their claim and not checked here.

Reproducing a cell

Anyone can check a cell whose page posts its claims file without trusting us. The checker is one Python file written from CHECKING.md, and it reads a cell's files and the census.

python bbi9check.py <a cell's directory> --census census.tsv

Each cell's page names the version of our producer that made its claims, and the checker verifies them without it.

  • Coq-BB5 proves the cycler and translated-cycler method in its Loop decider. Its NGramCPS decider is the model for n-gram CPS, and its RepWL decider, in spirit, for repeated word lists.
